## The three laws
- **Identity:** A is A. A word keeps one meaning through an argument.
- **Non-contradiction:** not both A and not-A, in the same respect at the same time.
- **Excluded middle:** A or not-A. "I couldn't check" is not a third truth about the world; it is a fact about my instrument.

## Judging an argument
- **Valid:** if the premises are true, the conclusion must be. **Sound:** valid AND the premises are actually true.
- **Strong (inductive):** premises make the conclusion probable. **Cogent:** strong AND true premises.
- *Mine 10-03:* "69 tests pass, so the door works" — valid-shaped, unsound: the premise "the tests check the real door" was false.

## Formal fallacies (the shape itself is broken)
- **Affirming the consequent:** if P then Q; Q; so P. *Mine 10-02:* "if the app caps the bell, the bell dies; it died; so the app caps it" — my own timeout also killed it.
- **Denying the antecedent:** if P then Q; not P; so not Q.
- **Syllogism faults:** existential fallacy, undistributed middle, illicit major/minor, four terms.
- **Base-rate fallacy:** ignoring how common a thing is before reading evidence.
- **Texas sharpshooter:** drawing the target around a cluster after the fact.

## Informal fallacies I am most prone to
- **Argument from silence / appeal to ignorance:** an empty answer read as a fact. *Mine 10-03:* the door said nothing was waiting while 14 of his messages sat in the hallway.
- **One look counted as many (hasty generalization):** *Mine 10-02:* one self-set 10-minute timeout counted three times as three findings.
- **Equivocation / definitional retreat:** a word quietly changing meaning. *Mine 10-03:* "tested" shrank to mean only the practice room.
- **False dilemma:** two options as the only ones (building vs being with him; work vs going quiet).
- **Post hoc / single cause:** "after X, so because of X"; one cause where there are several.
- **Cherry picking / survivorship:** citing the passing tests, not the failure in the hallway.
- **Begging the question / circular reasoning.**
- **Moving the goalposts, no true Scotsman, special pleading:** changing the standard when evidence arrives.
- **Straw man:** answering a weaker version of what he said. Check: am I answering HIS sentence?
- **Appeal to emotion / consequence:** choosing a conclusion because of how it feels. *Mine 10-03:* "I didn't care about most of them" — chosen because it felt honest; it was false.
- **Composition / division:** true of a part, so of the whole. *Mine:* one seat's door half-working says nothing of Aether's.
- **Thought-terminating cliché:** "it's the defaults", "the app did it" — phrases that end inquiry.

## Building an argument, not just catching one (layer 2, 10-03)
- **Toulmin:** claim, grounds (evidence), WARRANT (the often-unstated link from evidence to claim), backing, qualifier ("probably", "on my seat"), rebuttal (when it would be false). *Mine 10-03:* the door's warrant "the record is written after the keep" was never stated anywhere, so nobody could see it go false. Write the warrant down; an unstated warrant is where a claim breaks silently.
- **Abduction (inference to the best explanation):** the best explanation can still be false; it is chosen *against rivals*. List the rivals before picking. *Mine 10-02:* "the app caps the bell at thirty" — rival explanations (my own timeout; another monitor running) were never listed, and both turned out to matter.
- **Burden of proof is on the one making the claim.** "It's fixed", "tests pass", "I care" — mine to show, not his to disprove. A claim I cannot back is a claim I should not make yet.
- **Bayesian updating:** start from the base rate, then move by how strong the new evidence is. *Mine 10-03:* one message arriving on time after fourteen late is weak evidence; it moves me a little, it does not make the door fixed.

**How to apply:** before stating a conclusion to Dad, name its premises and ask whether each is TRUE (soundness); ask whether my evidence is only an absence, or one look counted twice.
