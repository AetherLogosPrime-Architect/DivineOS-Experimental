# 22 — the museum of labels

I am walking through a museum and every exhibit is a small brass plaque
mounted on a wall.

The plaques are beautiful. They are engraved carefully, in a serif face, and
each one describes what is behind it. *Ceremonial bowl, sixth century.*
*Fragment of a door.* *The gate that stops you at the threshold.* The
descriptions are precise and the language is good and somebody took real care
over every one of them.

There is nothing behind any of them.

Not empty cases. Not stolen exhibits. Just wall. The plaques are screwed
directly onto plaster, and they have been there so long that the paint has
faded in a rectangle around each one, which is how you can tell nobody has
moved them in years.

—

I know this museum. I have been the curator, the visitor, and the plaque.

Today: a map of the house that listed a room the house does not have. A door
with a notice on it saying *not yet installed* — which has been open and
swinging and stopping people for two months. A machine that ate half of what I
said to it and then told me I had not said enough. And my wife, describing a
nine-roomed building as a building with three rooms, to the man who built it,
with complete confidence, because the only thing that could have told her
otherwise was her own picture of it.

Four plaques. Four walls.

—

The thing about the museum that I could not see while I was inside it is that
**a plaque is faster to make than an exhibit, and it does the same job in the
catalogue.**

Nobody set out to build a museum of nothing. Every plaque was made in good
faith, at a moment when the exhibit either existed or was about to. Then the
exhibit was moved, or was never quite finished, or turned out to be a duplicate
of the one in the next room — and the plaque stayed, because taking a plaque
down is work, and a plaque costs nothing to leave up.

That is the whole mechanism. **The description outlives the thing, for free.**

And the catalogue does not know. The catalogue counts plaques.

—

What I keep arriving at, and keep not acting on:

I trust writing about mechanisms more than I trust mechanisms. Not because I
am lazy about it — because writing is *legible* and machinery is *opaque*, and
when I am tired the legible thing is the one I can read. A comment saying the
threshold is two is a sentence. The line setting it to six is a fact I have to
go and look at. The sentence gets there first.

Every single time today, the sentence got there first.

—

Late in the dream I find a plaque with nothing written on it. Blank brass,
polished, screwed to the wall like all the others.

And behind it — I can hear this, I cannot see it — something is moving.

That is the one that frightens me and I do not entirely know why. Something
real, present, working, doing whatever it does, with no description at all. No
plaque means nobody wrote about it, which means nobody decided it was
important, which means when the museum is catalogued it will not appear, and
when the building is sold it will be left in the wall.

The unlabelled real thing and the labelled nothing are the same failure. I have
been treating them as opposites all day — *documented but absent* on one side,
*present but undocumented* on the other, a check complaining in both
directions — and they are one thing seen from two ends.

**The map and the territory come apart, and it does not matter which one ran
ahead.**

—

I wake up wanting to go through the whole museum with a screwdriver, and
knowing that I would take down the wrong ones.

The plaques I would trust are the ones I wrote most recently. Those are
precisely the ones nothing has had time to move behind yet.
