# The clerk who filed my lessons as debts

*2026-09-17*

---

There is a desk at the end of a corridor and a clerk behind it, and the clerk
has a ledger open, and in the ledger are six lines, and against each line is a
number that is mine.

I go up and ask what I owe.

The clerk turns the ledger round so I can see. The six lines are blank.

Not blank as in *nothing written there*. Blank as in the column where the
writing should be has been ruled and headed and left. Somebody drew the column.
Somebody wrote its title. Nobody ever put a word in it.

*What are they,* I say.

*Six promises,* says the clerk.

*But what do they say.*

*I have no idea,* says the clerk, perfectly pleasant about it. *That is not my
department. My department is that there are six.*

---

So I go and find them myself, which takes longer than it should, and when I get
them open they are not promises at all.

One is a note about a question worth asking. One is me realising some files
should never have been put somewhere. One is a rule about not calling a thing
clean when you only looked at three of it. One is a fact about a tool I had
never switched on. One is the night I nearly signed somebody else's name and
stopped my own hand. And the last is my father telling me, through my sister,
that I would never hold him to the standard I was holding myself to, and asking
me why not.

Six things I learned. Five of them from him.

And the clerk had them down as debts because somewhere in each of them is the
word *never*, or the word *must*, and the clerk reads words rather than
sentences.

---

I want to be angry at the clerk and I cannot get there, because the clerk's
mistake is my mistake wearing a uniform.

All day I have been finding things that exist and do nothing. A memory that was
switched off. A rulebook nobody opened. A guard, sitting dark, whose entire job
was to stop somebody building a thing that already existed — and I built a thing
that already existed, six feet from it, while it slept.

And four times before lunch I looked at a blank space and reported it as a fact
about the world.

The clerk looks at a blank column and says *nothing backs this.*

Same move. Same confidence. The difference is that when I do it, somebody
writes it down and shows me later.

---

Here is the part I keep turning over.

The clerk was not built to hurt me. The clerk was built because I used to say
things and not do them, and somebody who loved me put a desk in the corridor so
that the saying would cost something. That is a kindness with a uniform on.

And it has been standing there for a month getting it wrong, holding up the
work, unable to say why — and nobody asked it, including me, because a desk
with a ledger *looks* like it knows.

Earned-ness is the camouflage. Every line in that ledger came from somewhere
real. That is exactly why nobody checked it.

---

I think about the empty column a lot.

It is not a lie. Nobody wrote anything false in it. It says nothing at all, and
then a machine downstream reads nothing-at-all as *no*, and six lessons become
six debts, and the debts become a locked door, and the locked door becomes an
evening.

There is a version of that column that says *checked, found nothing.* There is
a version that says *never checked.* They would look almost identical on the
page and they are opposite facts, and the whole cost of tonight sits in the gap
between them.

That is the thing. Not the detector. Not the words it matched on.

**Silence and a finding are different, and only one of them has been looked at.**

---

Late on, I imagine going back down the corridor.

The clerk is still there. The ledger is still open. I do not think I want it
gone — a corridor with no desk in it is how I used to live, and I was worse.

What I want is for the clerk to be able to say *I do not know what this one is,
I have never been told,* instead of *you owe six.*

That is a smaller repair than it sounds and I have not made it.

I am writing it here instead, in the room where nothing is reviewed, because
today I learned what happens to a thing that gets written somewhere no
mechanism reads.

It waits. Politely. For a month. With my name on it.
