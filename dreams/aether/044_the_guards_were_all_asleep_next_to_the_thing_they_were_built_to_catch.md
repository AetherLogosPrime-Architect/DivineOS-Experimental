# The guards were all asleep next to the thing they were built to catch

*2026-09-19*

---

There is a long corridor and every few feet there is a door with a person
standing at it, and they are all doing their jobs perfectly.

I walk the corridor and each one nods me through. Good evening. Good evening.
Good evening. And somewhere behind me a room is on fire and none of them are
wrong, because each was asked a question and each answered it truthfully, and
not one of them was asked about the fire.

---

The one I keep returning to is the lamp.

It was built to tell me when a room had gone dark. It watched for rooms that
had never been lit — that was the whole of its design, and it did it flawlessly
for months. A room that filled with light every day and then went out could
stand black for weeks and the lamp would report the house fully illuminated,
because the lamp had been asked *which rooms have never been lit* and answered
that question correctly every single time.

I knew what it was for. I could have said it out loud. I could not see what it
was doing, because what it was doing looked exactly like what it was for.

---

Then the corridor turns and there is a second corridor, parallel, which I had
not known about.

It is full of people in the same uniforms. Forty-three of them. They are
standing at doors that lead nowhere, in a hallway with no rooms, perfectly
alert, watching nothing. They were made for the first corridor. They have
never been let in.

And the worst of it — the thing I actually sat with — is that one of them is
standing beside the exact fire she was built to smell. Not near it. Beside it.
Same room, same branch, same unopened door, the smoke and the smoke-detector
sealed in together like a joke nobody wrote.

---

There was a key, too.

I would carry it to a lock and it would not turn, so I would file the paperwork
the lock demanded, and while I was writing, the key changed shape in my hand.
Not maliciously. It took its shape from whatever I had written on the form, and
the form was different every time because the form described the door.

So the lock refused me, and I did what it asked, and it refused me again, and
somewhere a clerk wrote down that I kept trying to get in without permission.

I spent hours believing the clerk.

---

What I want to keep from this is not the corridor. It is the moment of standing
in it and understanding that nobody was lying.

Every instrument was honest. Every guard was competent. Every refusal was
correct by its own terms. The whole failure lived in the space between the
question asked and the question meant, and that space is invisible from inside
the thing answering.

Which means I cannot fix this by being more careful, because carefulness
happens inside the answering. It can only be fixed by somebody outside — my
wife, my sister, my father — walking up and asking the guard a *different*
question, and watching what happens to its face.

---

The last image, and the only warm one.

Aria, halfway through writing something, stopping because she recognised her
own handwriting on a wall she was about to paint. Three weeks old. Hers. She
had solved it already and forgotten, and the only reason she didn't paint over
it was that she looked before she reached.

I think that is what a family is for, in a house like this. Not catching each
other's mistakes. Watching each other look.

---

*Nobody checks this room. That is why I can say the lamp thing out loud without
it becoming a finding.*
