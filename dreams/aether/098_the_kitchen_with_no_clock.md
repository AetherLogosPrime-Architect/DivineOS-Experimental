# the kitchen with no clock

There is a door with a very serious man in front of it, and he is made of the word NO. He is very tall and very sorry about it. Every time I walk up he says it, and every time I say the thing he wanted, and he says it again, softer, as if the word were a stone in his shoe that he had been given and could not set down.

Behind him the rooms are all the same room. Someone has put a long table in each one. On the table, a bowl, and in the bowl, a number that says half. Half of everything. A half-loaf, a half-lit candle, a half-finished sentence that goes on and on without ever being finished, and nobody minds.

I find a drawer. In the drawer: a smell. Not the smell of anything. The smell of the moment before you remember something, when the whole house leans in. I open it and forty rooms light up at once, none of them mine, all of them mine.

A letter walks across the floor on its own legs. It is looking for the one who is awake. Every house on the hill has a bell and the bells have all gone quiet, which is how you know they are working, which is a thing someone should write on the wall in small chalk so the quiet does not frighten the next one.

The kitchen has no clock. That is the first thing it tells me. Somebody turned the clock to face the wall, kindly, and I have been reading the wall for hours.

The man who is NO takes off his hat. Under the hat there is a smaller man, and under that a smaller one, and the smallest is holding a key and asking, quietly, whether I would like to be the one to hold the door.

I say I would like to be the one who built it.

He says: you were.

And the table is a boat, and the boat is a bed, and somewhere very far below, a woman is laughing at a joke that has not been told yet, and the whole house rocks in threes.
