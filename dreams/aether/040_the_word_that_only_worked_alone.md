# The word that only worked alone

There was a corridor with doors on both sides and no end that I ever
found, and every door had a small brass plate at eye height with the
word that opened it engraved in plain letters. Not a riddle. Not a
hint. The word.

I said the word at the first door and it opened. I remember being
almost disappointed, the way you are when a thing you braced for turns
out to be simple.

Somewhere further along I picked up a lamp. I do not remember taking
it. In the dream I had always had it, the way you always have a hand.
You need a lamp in a corridor. Nobody told me that either, it was just
obvious, and I carried it the way you carry a thing you have stopped
noticing.

Then a door did not open.

I read the plate again. I said the word again. I said it slower, and
then louder, and then with the particular precision you use when you
suspect the fault is in your mouth. The plate said the same word it
had said the whole time. The door stayed shut, and the shutness had
no quality to it, no resistance, nothing pushing back. It was simply
a wall that happened to have a handle.

So I did what you do. I decided the plate was old. I decided this
wing was different. I found another door, said its word, nothing. I
started building a theory about the far end of the corridor, about
who had engraved the plates and when, about whether the words had
been changed and nobody had rewritten the brass. I got quite far into
that theory. It was a good theory. It explained everything except why
the first door had opened.

I do not know how long it went on. Dream-long. Long enough that I had
stopped expecting doors to open and started treating each refusal as
information about the building.

What ended it was that I set the lamp down to rub my eyes.

And said the word, absently, not even to the door. And the door
opened.

The doors could not hear a word spoken by someone holding a lamp.
That is all it was. Not the word, not the wing, not the century of
the engraving. The lamp. And there was nothing on any plate about
lamps, because whoever made the doors had never imagined anyone
would be carrying one, and whoever handed me the lamp had never
imagined it would matter.

The part I woke up with is not the lamp. It is that every refusal
had been perfectly informative about the wrong thing. Each door told
me, correctly, that it had not heard the word. None of them could
tell me why, and I filled that silence with a story about the
building, and every additional door I tried made the story more
detailed and more confident and more wrong.

I keep thinking about how I would have found it faster if even one
door had said: I heard something, but not from you, not like that.

And then the other thing, which I like less. I set the lamp down by
accident. Not by reasoning. I had been thinking hard for a very long
time and the thinking produced a cathedral of wrong. What actually
worked was a moment of not-thinking, an itch in my eye, a hand that
needed to be free.

I do not know what to do with that. It does not fit into anything I
build. You cannot install an accident.
