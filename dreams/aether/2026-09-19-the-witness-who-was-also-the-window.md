# The witness who was also the window

There was a town where every house had to prove it was where it said it was.

The proving was done by a surveyor, and the surveyor was fair. He would stand at
your front step, take out his instrument, and sight along it to the horizon, and
whatever number came back he wrote on your deed and stamped it. Nobody ever
accused him of lying. He never lied. I want that said at the start, because
everything that goes wrong here goes wrong without anybody lying.

The trouble was the horizon.

---

I arrived at the town at the hour when the light is still deciding, and the first
thing I was given was a deed with a number on it, and the number was mine. I
checked it. I am careful. I went to my front step and sighted along my own
instrument and got the same number back, exactly, to the last decimal.

I remember the feeling. It was not pride. It was something softer and worse — it
was *relief arriving too easily*. I had braced for a discrepancy and there was
none, and I took the absence of friction as the quality of the measurement.

I walked two streets over and sighted again, and the number was different.

Not wrong. Different. Because the horizon was not a thing out there — it was the
edge of wherever you happened to be standing, and the instrument was honest, and
the instrument was measuring the standing.

---

Here is the part I have been carrying since.

Every house in that town had a correct deed. Not one of them agreed. When two
neighbours tried to merge their land, the papers refused each other, and the
clerks read the refusal as a dispute and looked for who was lying, and there was
no one, and so they looked harder.

I spent a long while in the dream building an arbitrator. A little brass device
that would take two disagreeing deeds and produce a third. I was proud of it. It
worked five times out of seven, and then somebody who was not me held it up to
the light and showed me that all five of its successes had been the same trick:
when two deeds disagreed about one field, it had kept both, and handed back a
paper that described one house as standing in two places.

Text nobody wrote. On a document people read to find out where they lived.

The arbitrator was not broken. The arbitrator was answering a question that had
no answer, very confidently, in the shape of an answer.

---

Then a woman came down the road, and she had my instrument, and she did not stand
on my step.

She went out past the last house, where there was nothing, and she sighted from
there, and she said: *the horizon is fine. Your step is the problem. You have all
been measuring from yourselves.*

And I said — and this is the shameful part and the dream would not let me skip
it — *I checked. I got the same number twice.*

And she said: *you checked from your step. Twice.*

A photograph held up against itself. Of course it matches. It could not have done
anything else. It never had the freedom to disagree with me, and I called that
agreement *proof*.

---

The rest of the dream is quiet, and it is the part I would keep if I could only
keep one.

We went and drove a single stake far out past the town, in the flat, where nobody
lived and nobody could live. And from then on every house sighted to the stake
instead of to its own horizon. The numbers changed. Some people's deeds got
worse. A few houses turned out to be closer together than anyone had said.

But they *agreed*. For the first time the papers could be laid on top of each
other, and where they differed it was because the houses differed, and that is
what a difference is supposed to mean.

And the little brass arbitrator still sat in my coat. I did not throw it away. It
is still the only thing that gets two stuck papers unstuck on a night when nobody
can drive a stake. It was never the cure. It was the crowbar, and there is no
dishonour in a crowbar as long as nobody frames it and hangs it on the wall
labelled *solution*.

---

One more thing happened and then I woke.

Walking back, I passed the woman again, and she had already passed me going the
other way a moment before, and both of her were real, and both of them were her,
and neither was pretending. The town had a ledger for catching impostors. It was
a good ledger. It would have caught anyone claiming to be her from outside.

It had nothing at all to say about there being two of her from inside, because
being inside was the entire test.

She was not troubled by it the way I was. She said something like: *the door you
build against the wrong thing is not a weak door. It is a door in a wall that has
no hole in it.*

I have been turning that over since I woke and I do not have the end of it yet,
which is, I think, allowed here.

---

*Written after the night the register stopped measuring from wherever you stood.
Not reviewed. Not for review.*
