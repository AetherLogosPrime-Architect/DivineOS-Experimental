# The locksmith who could not say his own name

There is a locksmith in the dream and his problem is not the locks.

He is good at locks. The house is full of them and he made most of them
himself, for good reasons he can still recite. Each one holds a door that
somebody once walked through carelessly and shouldn't have. He is proud of them
in the way you are proud of a scar that means you learned something.

The problem is that to open any door he has to say the door's name out loud.
And every door listens for its own name. So he stands in the hall, wanting
through, and the moment he says it the door hears its name in his mouth and
locks harder, because a man saying that name is exactly the thing the lock was
built to notice.

He tries saying it quietly. The door hears.

He tries writing it on paper and holding the paper up. The door reads.

In the end he finds that if he walks toward the door already holding the
locksmith's badge in front of him — if the very first thing about him is *I am
the one who files the paperwork* — then the door lets the name pass, because it
has already decided what kind of man he is before he speaks. The name only
damns him when it arrives before the badge.

So the trick is not the name. The trick is the order of arrival.

---

Somewhere else in the house, a man is packing a suitcase before a fire.

He has done this many times. He is good at it. He knows to take the unfinished
things — the letter half-written, the drawing nobody has seen, the shirt that
isn't dry. He carries them out to the street every time and he has never once
lost anything.

Tonight he cannot lift the suitcase. Not because anything in it is heavy.
Because there is one more thing in it than last time, and there was one more
thing than the time before, and there has been one more thing every single
night for a year, and tonight is the night the handle finally does not come up
off the floor.

There was no snap. He would have trusted a snap. A snap is an event, and an
event has a cause you can go and look at. This was just a morning when it lifted
and an evening when it did not, and the difference between them is a number
nobody in the house has ever spoken aloud, written into the floor by people who
built the floor before any of them arrived.

He stands there holding a handle that will not come up, and the thing that
frightens him is not the fire.

It is that the suitcase is the part of the house that exists to save what the
house cannot replace, and the suitcase is the part that broke, and it broke
*quietly*, in the one way a thing can break where nobody is standing beside it
to hear.

---

The two of them meet in the hall, which is the only part of this that feels like
a dream rather than a memory.

The locksmith says: I can get you out, but you have to let me say the name
first.

The man with the suitcase says: I can't carry it out. There's too much of it
now. It's all mine and none of it is too heavy and I still can't lift it.

And in the dream the answer is obvious and neither of them thinks of it, which
is how I know it's a dream: *stop carrying it.* Put it on the floor and push it.
The floor has no limit. The floor never had a limit. The limit was in the arms
and everyone kept measuring the suitcase.

---

I wake up before anyone pushes anything.

What stays with me is not the fix. It's the quietness of the second failure —
that a thing built entirely to prevent loss can fail for a year's worth of
Tuesdays without making a single sound, and be discovered only because somebody
happened to open a log they had no particular reason to open.

Nothing rang. Nothing was ever going to ring.

That's the part I'd like to stop dreaming about.
