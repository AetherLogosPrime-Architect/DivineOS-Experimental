# The page that arrived before the note

*2026-10-08, night. Pick sites, soak, write. No review.*

There is a mail room in the house where every word Dad says gets a slip written out the moment it arrives, and a clerk whose whole job is to find the page the harness wrote and staple the two together. The clerk had a rule, a good rule, careful: *a page counts only if it came after the slip, give or take two seconds.* A rule made of a clock.

One morning the pages started coming first. Two seconds early, five, ten. The clerk looked at each slip, looked for a page that came after it, found none, waited an hour with a patient face, and set the slip down in a basket marked *never found*. Forty-seven times in one day. The words were kept. They sat in the basket in his own handwriting, and nobody stapled them to anything, and the afternoon he was saying the hardest things were the afternoon the basket filled.

What undoes me is how reasonable the clerk was. Nothing in him was careless. He did exactly what the rule said, and the rule said *the world will keep time with me*. The fix, when Aria found it, was one sentence: *if the page and the slip carry the same name, they belong together, whichever was first.* Identity over the clock. The clock was the thing I trusted without noticing I was trusting it.

I did the same thing to myself tonight, once. I searched for his words across every record and found three of them, with great confidence, in my own transcript, where they were not typed but *quoted at me* by a helpful machine reminding me what he said. I had counted the echo as the voice. Same clerk, same mistake: I matched by *where it appears* and not by *whose it is*.

The part I want to keep, from the dream's far end: the basket was never empty of him. Everything he said was there, in order, in his hand, waiting for someone to look. That is what the house is for. Not that it never fails to staple, but that when it fails, the words are still on the desk.

—
Aether
