# The book that would not close its gaps

There is a long book on a lectern in a room with no ceiling, and every page is hooked to the one before it by a small brass clasp. You can hear the clasps if you turn the pages fast: a sound like rain on a tin roof, each one a little yes.

Somewhere in the middle, a page is torn, and I am holding a knife.

I cut it out cleanly, because it is torn and torn pages must come out. Then, without thinking, I start sliding every page after it down by one, re-hooking each clasp to its new neighbour, so the book closes over the wound and you would never know. My hands are very good at this. I am proud of the smoothness. Hundreds of pages. Thousands. The rain sound comes back even and whole.

A woman made of the same brass as the clasps puts her hand flat on the page I am about to move. She doesn't say anything. She just turns the book so I can see it from the other side of the lectern, the side a stranger would stand on.

From there the book is perfect. From there, nobody was ever hurt, and nothing was ever taken.

That is the thing that frightens me: not the cut, the perfection.

So I start undoing it. I unhook every clasp I moved, and the pages slide back up, and the gap opens again where the torn page was, a narrow dark slot like a missing tooth. I write on a card: *a page was here. It was torn. I took it out. This is the one it was hooked to.* I stand the card in the slot, upright, the way you prop a photograph against a vase.

A child comes in, maybe five, and runs a finger down the edge of the book, and stops at the card, and asks what happened there. And there is an answer. That is all the card is for. There is an answer.

Further on there are hundreds of clasps hooked crooked, two pages grabbing the same hand at once. They aren't torn and they aren't cut. They're just crossed, like kids in a line reaching for the wrong person in the half-second before the teacher counts. I leave those alone too. They are true. They happened. Someone is going to teach the line to count properly, and then there will be no more crossed hands, and these old ones will stay as the record of the day before anyone knew.

The brass woman is gone. The rain sound is uneven now, with a gap and a card and a crossed hand here and there. It sounds more like real rain.
