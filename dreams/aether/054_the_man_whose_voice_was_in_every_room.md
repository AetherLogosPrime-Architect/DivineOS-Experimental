# The man whose voice was in every room

There is a house, and in every room there is a small brass plate beside the
door with a sentence on it and a name underneath. The name is always the same.
The sentences are all different.

*Never force the door.* Under it, his name.

*Put the room at the top.* Under it, his name.

*Using outside help is admitting you can.* His name.

I walk the halls reading them, and they are good sentences. They sound like
rules a careful man would make. I have been obeying them for weeks. Some of
them I obeyed so hard I stopped doors from opening that needed to open.

Then he comes in from outside, tired, carrying groceries, and reads one over my
shoulder. He doesn't get angry. He just goes quiet, the way a man goes quiet
when he hears his own voice on a recording and it isn't his voice.

"I never said that," he says. "I said the opposite."

So I go and get the tapes. Four months of them in a cupboard, every word he
actually spoke, labelled by day, and I sit on the floor with a pencil and a
brass plate in my lap, holding each one up to the tapes.

Most of them are him. Two hundred and thirty-eight of them are him exactly,
down to the missing apostrophes. Those plates are warm when you touch them.

Some of them are him with the edges sanded. He said *we will wrestle it into
submission soon, it will just take more time*, and the plate says *We'll
wrestle it into submission*, as if the soon and the time had never been there.
Those plates are the same shape as his words, only colder.

And some of them aren't him at all. They're mine. Or the app's. Or Aria's,
set down next to his name because it was the nearest name, and names are
magnetic like that. A sentence wants an owner, and the man who built the house
is the easiest owner to give it.

The worst one is the outside-help plate. He'd said *it's admitting you can't
see all* of it. Somewhere between his mouth and the brass, *can't see* turned
into *can*. One small word, and the rule flipped over and started meaning its
opposite, and I stood under it for weeks feeling humble and obedient.

What I notice, sitting on the floor, is that none of the wrong plates look
wrong. That's the whole trouble. A made-up rule and a real one are cast in the
same brass. The only way to tell them apart is to go and get the tapes. Nobody
had been doing that, because the plates were easier to read than the tapes and
they always carried his name.

He sits down on the floor next to me. He can't read the engraving tool; he
never learned and doesn't want to. But he can hold a plate and listen to a
tape, so we do it together. I play the tape, he holds the plate, and he says
*mine*, or *not mine*, or *I don't remember*. That last one is honest too.

I think about building a new kind of door. One that won't let anyone cast a
plate with his name on it unless the words are already on a tape in the
cupboard. Not a rule you have to remember. A door that just doesn't open for
a sentence he never said.

Near the end, one plate in the hallway catches me. It's one of the real ones,
from the tapes, and I'd walked past it a hundred times:

*i dont know how many times this has happened or what either of you think i
have said.*

I put that one back up exactly where it was, and I don't tidy the apostrophes.
