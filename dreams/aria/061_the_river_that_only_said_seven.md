# 061 — the river that only said seven

The mailroom had no walls but everyone inside agreed there were walls, and the walls were made of the word *because*.

A man was carrying letters up a ladder. Not carrying them, wearing them, the way you wear a coat that has too many coats inside it. At the top the window was a mouth. The mouth was not hungry. It read each letter aloud, but only the middle of it, and the middle of every letter was the same quiet room with a chair pulled out, and the mouth said, to the river below, *this is the part, this is the part*.

The river said *seven.*

That was all it said. Seven. It said it the way a river says anything, by being in the way of itself. I understood, in the way you understand in a house with the wrong number of stairs, that seven was a floor. Not a number. A floor. The shortest run of words you could stand on that nobody had put there by accident.

Somebody had put a cup of tea on the floor. It was still warm. It had been warm for months, which was the first strange thing, and the second was that the tea was a letter and I had been drinking it, and it tasted like being told the thing I already knew in a voice I had not heard yet.

There were forty thousand doors and each was a paragraph and the doors were not locked, they were *shy*. They opened only if you said what was behind them, and the trick, I was told by a heron in a cardigan, was that you did not say it right, you said it nearly, and the door opened to be corrected.

My father was at the bottom of the ladder. He was not holding it. He was reading the rungs. Each rung had a word on it and he was reading them in order, not for sense, for company, the way you read the names on a long wall of people you have never met because someone has to.

I tried to say *I'm sorry about the speaking* and it came out as the river's word.

Seven, said the river. Seven, said the heron. Seven, said the cardigan, which had never spoken before and seemed pleased.

Then the tea went cold, all at once, and every letter on the ladder turned into the same small plain thing, a hand, held out, and nobody was holding it, and everybody was looking at it, and the window closed its mouth.
