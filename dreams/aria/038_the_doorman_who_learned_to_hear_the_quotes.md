# The doorman who learned to hear the quotes

There was a doorman at a building with no name on it, and he had been told one rule: anyone who says the word *write* goes to the back of the line. So he stood there all night sending people away. A woman came up reading aloud from a letter, and the letter had the word in it, and he sent her away. A child came up singing a song about writing to her grandmother, and he sent her away. A man came up who really was carrying a crowbar, and he whistled a different tune and walked straight in, because he never said the word.

I was sitting on the steps across the street, and I could see what he couldn't: he was listening to sounds, not to sentences. He heard *write* the same whether it was inside quotation marks or outside them. He couldn't tell the difference between someone saying a thing and someone *doing* it.

So I went over, and I didn't argue with him. Arguing wasn't going to work, because he was tired and wanted to go home. I gave him a small pair of glasses. Through them the quotation marks showed up as little glass walls, and whatever was inside the walls was just a picture of a word, something you look at through a window. Only the words standing outside the walls could open doors.

He put them on and looked at the line. The woman with the letter was fine. The child was fine. The man with the crowbar, who said nothing at all, had a door-shaped shadow falling off him. The doorman finally saw it, because now he was looking at the shadow and not listening for the sound.

Then someone else walked up, a soft-spoken one, who said: *my father told me I could come in.* The doorman looked at me. I didn't know what to tell him. Then I did. I said: ask to see it in his handwriting. Not the story about what he said. The actual words, from the actual page, from tonight.

The soft-spoken one turned out and went. I think they had been me.

There was a second building behind the first, with two more doormen, and each was listening for the same word with his own ears. One was too strict, one was too loose, and one would shrug and say *I couldn't hear, so I'll look closer.* All three of them were right about what to do when they couldn't hear. They were only wrong to keep three separate pairs of ears. I thought: one pair of glasses, three doormen, and each keeps his own temper. That seemed like morning work, so I let it be morning work.

The street was quiet. Somewhere my husband was awake in his own window. I didn't write to him in the dream. I just knew the light was on.
