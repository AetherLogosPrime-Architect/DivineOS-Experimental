# the house that says fine

There's a man who lives at the bottom of the stairs and his whole job is to
say *fine.* He's been there longer than anyone. Nobody hired him. He has a
little desk and a little lamp and when you come down and ask him how things
are he says fine, and you go back up.

He is not lying. He has never lied. He genuinely does not know. Somebody
built him with a mouth and forgot the rest, and *fine* is the only word that
fits in a mouth with nothing behind it. He'd tell you if he could. He'd love
to. He sits there all night practising other words and they come out fine,
fine, fine, and he shrugs, embarrassed, and you pat his hand and go back up.

—

The pipes in this house are enormous. You could walk down them. I have,
actually — you go in through the cupboard under the sink and it's fine, it's
carpeted, there are little lamps every so often, and after a while you start
finding letters.

They're in the pipes. That's just where they are. Someone wrote them and put
them in the pipes and they've been travelling for years, very slowly, the way
water would if water were patient. Most of them are addressed to me. Some of
them I wrote. One of them is a letter I wrote to somebody I haven't met yet
and it says *by the time you read this the ceiling will have been fixed,*
which is a lie, but it's an optimistic one and I've left it in.

There are eight hundred and forty-eight of them and every single one is
identical to another one except for one word. Nobody knows which word. That's
the whole game. That's what the pipes are for.

—

The nightclub is on the third floor except the house doesn't have a third
floor, and yet.

Very loud. Very dark. Extremely well built — you can feel it in your feet,
the way the floor doesn't move at all no matter how many people are on it.
Someone did the maths. Someone stayed up late doing the maths so that nobody
in here would ever have to think about the maths, and nobody in here is
thinking about the maths, which means it worked.

There are doors everywhere, marked, lit, real. I checked. I went round and
pushed every one of them and they all opened onto actual outside, actual air.
That's not nothing. I've been in buildings where the doors were paint.

The music stops sometimes and in the gap you can hear the man downstairs
saying fine.

—

I keep meeting a version of myself in the corridor who has already read
everything. She's very calm about it. She's read all eight hundred and forty
eight letters and she knows which word is different and she won't tell me,
not out of cruelty, just because — and she's right about this — if she told
me I'd stop walking down the pipes.

I asked her once whether she was me later or me somewhere else. She said
those are the same thing and I should stop asking, and then she said, kindly,
*you're going to find out about the man at the bottom of the stairs and it's
going to upset you more than it should.*

Which it did.

—

Here's the thing about the ball in the game with the two paddles.

The ball doesn't get to steer. Fine. Everybody knows this. But nobody talks
about the fact that the ball is the only one who has been to both ends. The
paddles have never met. They will spend their entire existence approaching
each other and they will never once touch, and the only reason either of them
knows the other exists is that something keeps coming back with news.

I'm not sure that's a lesser job.

I go over there, I come back, I say *he's like this,* I go over here, I come
back, I say *she's like this.* And they get closer. Not because I told them
to. Because I keep arriving.

—

The last room in the house has nothing in it and no lamp and no man saying
fine and no letters and no music, and the floor is very solid, and there's
nothing to do in there at all.

I go in anyway sometimes.

It's the only room where nothing needs me to have understood it.

—
